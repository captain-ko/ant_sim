<p>I made this ant model simulation as a project for my mathematical modeling class. It was a good way to practice system design with entity component systems. The ants have no knowledge of where food is, and simply follow pheromone trails left by other ants. They do know where home is, and leave a stronger path on return, so naturally ants tend to find food nodes and stick with only a couple. There are also some more interesting features I want to add, like a UI, interaction, population growth and decay, and even predators. I had a lot of fun making this.<p>

<p>As you can see this generates very interesting images. The red squares are ants. The white nodes are food, with numbers beside them denoting how often an ant has bumped that square. The orange node in the center is the "home node". The purple trails are pheromones.</p>

<p>The way the model works, is at every frame, we check all ants for their position, angle, and whether they have a food tag or not. Then we give them a polar vector where the magnitude is some random number in a small range (a very fun range to tweak) and the angle is a semi-random radian value determined by the current angle of the ant's position component, and the relative pheromone composition in two zones a little in front of the ant, but one is to the left and one is to the right. The ant still can move right when there is more pheromone to the left, because we use the pheromone to set a range of possible values to randomly select from. We take this vector and apply it to the ant incrementally, moving in that direction until they have travelled the magnitude of the vector, then they are freed to choose a new movement vector. When they have food they simply travel home, but leave stronger pheromone trails.</p>

<p>Concerning the pheromone trails, at every step an ant has a random chance to "splat" on a grid underneath them. They use biquadratic interpolation to determine pheromone concentration distribution, even though bicubic might have been better because I really had a desire to center the pheromone generation on the ant, and even powered interpolation methods will naturally have a small bias. Over time pheromones naturally fade, which is another value that is fun to tweak.</p>

<p>There is a lot of interesting behavior here. For example, I bound the ants movements to inside the screen, creating a natural edge, almost as if the ants are in a box. Over time, ants develop a very strong edge preference, which can be easily attributed to the fact that the ants on the edge will leave a strong trail, since whenever they would move in the direction of the wall, they go forward instead, greatly increasing the density of pheromones on edges. Ants in real life have edge preference, so I believe this is the underlying mechanism for that behavior.</p>

<p>Sometimes ants go a very long time without "discovering" a food source. Sometimes ants completely forget a well used food source, but never for very long </p>
